# Facilitation 101 — foundations

## What facilitation is

A facilitator organizes a group so it reaches its own goals. Facilitation (from *facilitate* —
to make easier) is deliberate action to organize group work. It:
- raises the quality of group decision-making (analyze → find options → choose);
- builds and sustains a healthy climate (engagement, initiative, ownership of process & result);
- enables peer exchange and personal growth of participants.

**Content vs process.** Content (содержание) = the ideas, proposals and decisions the group
produces. Process (процесс) = *how* the group works. The facilitator acts on the process. You
cannot run a meeting impartially while personally invested in its subject, so content must be
separated from process. Controlling process without controlling content keeps the leader
neutral and lets the group reach the best, most viable decisions.

Role quadrant (content × process): **lecturer** = high content / low process; **trainer** =
high both; **facilitator** = low content / high process. The facilitator is "always above the
content".

## When facilitation fits — and when it doesn't

**Use it when:** the task is new or needs creativity; buy-in and shared ownership matter;
participants hold the relevant knowledge but can't solve it without an organized procedure;
they accept group-work rules; there's trust in the facilitator; responsibility for the result
is shared, responsibility for the procedure is the facilitator's.

**Avoid it when:** no clear goal; the answer is obvious / already decided; severe time pressure;
the majority will agree anyway and buy-in doesn't matter; participants lack knowledge and no
expert is present; unwillingness to take the goal or engage; no trust in the facilitator.

## IAF core competencies (the coverage map)

1. Plan group processes (goals, participants, methods, materials).
2. Partner with the client; understand real needs; manage expectations.
3. Create a safe, respectful space with explicit ground rules.
4. Run the process: time, dynamics, adapt techniques live, steer the discussion.
5. Handle conflict and distrust constructively.
6. Build consensus / find common ground.
7. Deliver results — capture outcomes into useful documents for action.

## Three complexity levels (calibration axis) + role aspects

| Level | Purpose | Facilitator's role | Design depth |
|-------|---------|--------------------|--------------|
| **Base meeting / working session** | Operational: status, coordination, input, a decision inside existing strategy | *Master of the process* — neutral arbiter & organizer | Lean: 1–2 techniques, tight timeboxes |
| **Strategy session / brainstorm (штурм)** | Strategy, vision, complex problems, change; hours–days | *Process architect* — designs a coherent arc | Deep: staged methodology, stakeholder mix |
| **Global dialogue / community participation** | Cross-org/sector/culture systemic dialogue | *Conductor* — inclusion, legitimacy, conflicting interests | Highest: intercultural, transparent, legitimate process |

Three role aspects a facilitator switches between by need: **process master** (wields the
techniques, reads the room), **dialogue architect** (designs the structure of interaction, the
sequence of questions), **lifecycle coordinator** (owns before / during / after). The goal is
never to be the hero of the session — it's to make oneself invisible and let the group own the
result. The facilitator is a servant of the process, not its owner.

## The goals pyramid (Global Dialog)

Four levels a session can target; each session names both a **rational goal** (what to achieve)
and an **existential goal** (the experience participants leave with):
1. **Shared understanding / common field** — align context, build a common picture.
2. **Search for alternatives** — generate options.
3. **Joint decision** — choose and commit.
4. **Roadmap** — turn the decision into concrete steps.

Don't aim higher than the inputs and time allow. Example (after a corporate conference):
rational = "a shared picture of the meanings/ideas from the conference"; existential =
"reinforce each other with information and conclusions, boost motivation".

## Client's role (роли заказчика) — A / B / C

- **A** — owns the final decision; gathers the group's wisdom, decides afterwards; may use a
  "golden like" to mark priorities. The facilitator tells the group up front the output feeds a
  leadership decision.
- **B** — wants true consensus that includes the leader; the leader participates as an equal,
  speaks last, controls verbal/non-verbal agreement signals so as not to dominate.
- **C** — only frames and sponsors the session; participants are autonomous; the leader has an
  advisory voice. Fits mature, self-managing teams.

The role shapes the design and especially the main-question verb (see `questions.md`).

## Situation types & the decision process

| Type | Decision-process steps it covers |
|------|----------------------------------|
| 1. Analysis of the situation/problem | Define goal; define constraints & criteria; define the essence of the problem; map cause-effect |
| 2. Define options / image of the future | Surface alternatives |
| 3. Choose the best option | Evaluate alternatives; final choice |

## Path to Action (Путь к Действию)

A planning & assessment tool. Five zones, each producing an agreement: **Vision of the future**
(image of the ideal future state) · **Problem** (nature & causes of the problem) · **Path
planning** (which path, and the order of moving through zones) · **Decision** (decisions each
will support) · **Implementation**. Use it to know at any moment what the group is focused on
now and what comes next. Common path shapes:
- **Problem-solving path** — critical problem, short timeframe.
- **Vision-realization path** — create something new / renew a system / rethink the goal.
- **Combined (problem → vision)** — when people must first understand what isn't working.
- **Combined (vision → problem)** — when obstacles to the vision must be cleared first.

## Divergence ↔ convergence, group roles

Two thinking modes the facilitator alternates: **divergent** (generate many varied ideas) and
**convergent** (logically evaluate and pick the best). Don't ask closed questions during
divergence. Typical roles in a group to balance: Organizer, Critic, Idea generator, Expert,
Supporter.

## Facilitator beliefs & traits (working stance)

People are smart and want to act effectively; the group can out-decide an individual; everyone's
voice matters regardless of rank; form matters as much as content; people commit to what they
helped create; a resisting group is energy, not catastrophe. Core traits: listen and hear;
transmit information precisely; structure time and information; ask the right questions; share
the "win" with the group; accept others' views without imposing your own; distinguish the
general from the particular and summarize. The facilitator need not be the subject expert, nor
do all the work alone.
